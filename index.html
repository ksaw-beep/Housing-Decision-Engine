    How This Decision Is Calculated

    Tap to expand

    Every number below is driven by your inputs — nothing is hardcoded. Change an input and re-analyze to see how the numbers shift.

    1. Upfront Costs
    Down payment + closing costs. This is the cash you commit on day one. A renter keeps this cash and invests it — so it's a real cost of ownership, not just a "transfer."
    Run analysis to see your numbers
    2. Monthly Cost of Owning
    Mortgage P&I + property tax + insurance + maintenance + HOA + PMI or FHA MIP (if applicable). All of these flow into the monthly cost — not just the mortgage payment.
    —
    3. Investment Impact (Renter's Side)
    A renter can invest two things: (a) the upfront cash not spent on the home, and (b) the monthly savings if owning costs more. Both grow at your investment return rate over your time horizon. This is what makes renting and investing sometimes competitive with ownership.
    —
    4. Exit Costs
    Agent commissions and transaction fees come out of your home's value when you sell. These are subtracted from equity before computing your net position — a step most simple calculators skip.
    —
    5. Net Wealth Comparison — the recommendation
    Buy net wealth = (equity built: down payment + principal paid + appreciation) minus (selling costs + total monthly ownership costs + closing costs paid).
    Rent net wealth = (investment gains: upfront cash invested + monthly savings invested at your return rate) minus (all rent paid over the period).
    The recommendation is whichever path produces the larger net position over your planned time horizon. Confidence level reflects how far you are from the tipping point.
    —

    Assumptions & Notes

    Tap to expand
    • Mortgage calculated using standard amortization formula
    • Closing costs are included as a % of home price (user-editable, default 3%) and added to the total upfront cash outflow for both break-even and wealth comparisons
    • Property tax uses state-level benchmark estimates that can vary significantly by county or municipality — override with your own number anytime
    • Conventional PMI is estimated from your credit score range when the down payment is below 20%, and is dropped automatically in the month the loan balance falls to 80% of the purchase price. FHA MIP (~0.55%/yr) runs 11 years with 10%+ down, or the life of the loan below 10% down. Planning estimates only, not a lender quote
    • Investment return assumption (user-adjustable, default 7%) is not guaranteed — it represents a broad market historical average for a diversified equity portfolio. Lower it to model a more conservative scenario
    • House hack rental income is adjusted for vacancy rate and "Operating Costs (% of rent)" — default 5% vacancy and 25% operating costs — so projections don't overstate net cash flow. These two controls reduce gross rental income to an estimated net figure
    • Move-out transition: from the year you move out, your unit is also rented (estimated at the average of your other units, after the same vacancy and operating-cost haircut) and you pay market rent elsewhere (your current rent, growing at the rent-growth rate) — the same rent the renter pays. Positive cash flow from the property reduces your cumulative cost
    • Affordability / DTI check is informational only — it does NOT determine mortgage approval. Lenders evaluate income, credit, assets, loan type, and many other factors. DTI benchmarks: ≤ 36% generally stronger; 36–45% may still qualify depending on credit and reserves; > 45% stretch territory — consult a lender
    • Home appreciation and rent growth are estimates, not guarantees
    • Maintenance estimated as a % of home value per year
    • Equity = down payment + principal paid + appreciation gain, minus exit costs at sale (agent commissions + transfer taxes + title/escrow fees, default 6%) applied to the projected home value at exit. This keeps the comparison apples-to-apples — the buy side only gets credit for equity you could actually realize by converting the home back to cash. On short horizons (< 5 years) this is one of the most impactful assumptions; on very long horizons it has less influence
    • The comparison is simulated month by month. The renter invests the full cash-to-close on day one. Each month, whichever path is cheaper invests the difference at the chosen return rate — so the renter's contributions shrink as rent grows, and a buyer whose payment is below rent invests too. Investment gains on both sides are taxed at the "Tax on Investment Gains" rate at exit; home-sale gains are treated as tax-free (the primary-residence exclusion)
    • Property tax, insurance, maintenance, and HOA grow at the "Ownership Cost Inflation" rate each year; mortgage principal & interest stay fixed
    • Not modeled: the mortgage-interest / property-tax deduction (most first-time buyers take the standard deduction; if you'd itemize, the true cost of owning is somewhat lower than shown), a VA funding fee, and points or rate buy-downs
    • Break-even compares cumulative net wealth including investment returns, scanning every month out to 10 years or your time horizon, whichever is longer
    • Sensitivity tipping points are the rate and price at which the recommendation itself flips (wealth impact at your horizon crosses zero), found by binary search — directionally accurate
    • Benchmarks shown next to inputs are planning guides based on long-run averages — not exact quotes for your specific home, lender, or insurer
    • This is a planning tool, not a lender quote or financial advice — consult a financial advisor for major decisions

    Help & FAQs

    What is PMI? What about FHA MIP or a VA funding fee?

    PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance) is an extra monthly charge on Conventional loans when your down payment is under 20%. It protects the lender — not you — and can usually be removed once you reach 20% equity.

    FHA MIP (Mortgage Insurance Premium) is the FHA equivalent. It's required on all FHA loans and often lasts 11 years, or the life of the loan if your down payment is under 10%.

    VA funding fee is a one-time upfront charge (~1.25–3.3% of the loan) on VA loans instead of monthly MI. This tool doesn't add it to your monthly payment — add it to your closing cost estimate if it applies.

    What is DTI and why does it matter?

    Debt-to-Income (DTI) is the share of your gross monthly income that goes to housing plus other debts (car, student loans, credit cards, etc.). Lenders use it to gauge whether you can comfortably take on a mortgage.

    Rough benchmarks: ≤ 36% generally stronger · 36–45% may still qualify with good credit/reserves · > 45% stretch territory. This tool shows DTI for reference only — it does not determine approval.

    What does "break-even" mean here?

    The break-even point is the month when buying's total net wealth overtakes renting-and-investing. Before that point, a renter who invested the down payment plus the monthly cash-flow difference would be ahead. After that point, equity + appreciation pull buying ahead.

    Our engine searches month-by-month out to 10 years, or your time horizon if it's longer. If no sustained crossover exists in that window, we say so.

    What is "wealth impact" over my time horizon?

    It's the dollar difference in your net financial position between buying and renting at the end of your planned ownership horizon. Both sides are computed the same way:

    • Buy side: equity built (down payment + principal paid + appreciation) minus all cash out (closing costs, monthly ownership costs net of rental income, selling costs at exit), plus after-tax gains on anything invested in months when owning was cheaper.
    • Rent side: after-tax investment gains on the cash you didn't spend on the home and on the monthly difference whenever renting is cheaper, minus all rent paid.

    A positive wealth impact means buying comes out ahead; negative means renting-and-investing does.

    What does the Confidence level (High / Medium / Low) mean?

    Confidence measures how robust the recommendation is to reasonable changes in your inputs — not how accurate the numbers are. It's derived from four signals:

    • Break-even vs. your horizon — how much cushion there is between the break-even point and how long you plan to stay.
    • Rate cushion — how far today's rate sits from the rate at which the recommendation would flip.
    • Price cushion — how far the home price sits from the price at which the call would flip.
    • Wealth-impact magnitude — how large the buy-vs-rent gap is at your time horizon.

    Interpretation:

    • High — the answer holds even if rates move ±1% or the home price drifts ±10%. You're well clear of the tipping points.
    • Medium — a ~1% rate swing or ~10% price change could flip this. Worth re-running with slightly different assumptions.
    • Low — essentially a coin flip. Small changes to rate, price, rent, or horizon can swap the recommendation. Save a second scenario and compare before committing.

    Low confidence isn’t a "bad" result — it means the two paths are genuinely close, and the tipping-point dial above will be near center to reflect that.

    What assumptions drive these numbers?

    The biggest levers are your interest rate, appreciation rate, investment return, time horizon, and rent growth. We also apply closing costs at purchase (default 3%) and exit costs at sale (default 6%).

    Why charge exit costs if you’re not the one selling yet? Every buyer is a future seller. For the buy-vs-rent wealth comparison to be apples-to-apples, the buy side has to net out the cost of eventually converting the home back to cash (agent commissions, transfer taxes, title fees) — otherwise it gets credit for equity you couldn’t actually realize. This matters most on short horizons; on 20+ year holds the impact is small.

    See the "Assumptions & Notes" section at the bottom of the analysis for the full list. Every number is driven by your inputs — nothing is hardcoded.
