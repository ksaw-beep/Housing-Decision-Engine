How This Decision Is Calculated

Every number below is driven by your inputs — nothing is hardcoded. Change an input and re-analyze to see how the numbers shift.

1. Upfront Costs
Down payment + closing costs. This is the cash you commit on day one. A renter keeps this cash and invests it — so it's a real cost of ownership, not just a "transfer."
Run analysis to see your numbers
2. Monthly Cost of Owning
Mortgage P&I + property tax + insurance + maintenance + HOA + PMI or FHA MIP (if applicable). All of these flow into the monthly cost — not just the mortgage payment.
—
3. Investment Impact (Renter's Side)
A renter can invest two things: (a) the upfront cash not spent on the home, and (b) the monthly savings if owning costs more. Both grow at your investment return rate over your time horizon. This is what makes renting and investing sometimes competitive with ownership.
—
4. Exit Costs
Agent commissions and transaction fees come out of your home's value when you sell. These are subtracted from equity before computing your net position — a step most simple calculators skip.
—
5. Net Wealth Comparison — the recommendation
Buy net wealth = (equity built: down payment + principal paid + appreciation) minus (selling costs + total monthly ownership costs + closing costs paid).
Rent net wealth = (investment gains: upfront cash invested + monthly savings invested at your return rate) minus (all rent paid over the period).
The recommendation is whichever path produces the larger net position over your planned time horizon. Confidence level reflects how far you are from the tipping point.

Help & FAQs

What is PMI? What about FHA MIP or a VA funding fee?

PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance) is an extra monthly charge on Conventional loans when your down payment is under 20%. It protects the lender — not you — and can usually be removed once you reach 20% equity.

FHA MIP (Mortgage Insurance Premium) is the FHA equivalent. It's required on all FHA loans and often lasts 11 years, or the life of the loan if your down payment is under 10%.

VA funding fee is a one-time upfront charge (~1.25–3.3% of the loan) on VA loans instead of monthly MI. This tool doesn't add it to your monthly payment — add it to your closing cost estimate if it applies.

What is DTI and why does it matter?

Debt-to-Income (DTI) is the share of your gross monthly income that goes to housing plus other debts (car, student loans, credit cards, etc.). Lenders use it to gauge whether you can comfortably take on a mortgage.

Rough benchmarks: ≤ 36% generally stronger · 36–45% may still qualify with good credit/reserves · > 45% stretch territory. This tool shows DTI for reference only — it does not determine approval.

What does "break-even" mean here?

The break-even point is the month when buying's total net wealth overtakes renting-and-investing. Before that point, a renter who invested the down payment plus the monthly cash-flow difference would be ahead. After that point, equity + appreciation pull buying ahead.

Our engine searches month-by-month out to 10 years. If no sustained crossover exists, we report "not within 10 years."

What is "wealth impact" over my time horizon?

It's the dollar difference in your net financial position between buying and renting at the end of your planned ownership horizon. Both sides are computed the same way:

  • Buy side: equity built (down payment + principal paid + appreciation) minus all cash out (closing costs, monthly ownership costs, selling costs at exit).
  • Rent side: investment gains on the cash you didn't spend on the home and on any monthly savings, minus all rent paid.

A positive wealth impact means buying comes out ahead; negative means renting-and-investing does.

What does the Confidence level (High / Medium / Low) mean?

Confidence measures how robust the recommendation is to reasonable changes in your inputs — not how accurate the numbers are. It's derived from four signals:

  • Break-even clarity — how soon (or whether) buying overtakes renting within 10 years.
  • Rate cushion — how far today's rate sits from the rate at which the monthly math would flip.
  • Price cushion — how far the home price sits from the price at which the call would flip.
  • Wealth-impact magnitude — how large the buy-vs-rent gap is at your time horizon.

Interpretation:

  • High — the answer holds even if rates move ±1% or the home price drifts ±10%. You're well clear of the tipping points.
  • Medium — a ~1% rate swing or ~10% price change could flip this. Worth re-running with slightly different assumptions.
  • Low — essentially a coin flip. Small changes to rate, price, rent, or horizon can swap the recommendation. Run a second scenario before committing.

Low confidence isn’t a "bad" result — it means the two paths are genuinely close, and the tipping-point dial above will be near center to reflect that.

What assumptions drive these numbers?

The biggest levers are your interest rate, appreciation rate, investment return, time horizon, and rent growth. We also apply closing costs at purchase (default 3%) and exit costs at sale (default 6%).

Why charge exit costs if you’re not the one selling yet? Every buyer is a future seller. For the buy-vs-rent wealth comparison to be apples-to-apples, the buy side has to net out the cost of eventually converting the home back to cash (agent commissions, transfer taxes, title fees) — otherwise it gets credit for equity you couldn’t actually realize. This matters most on short horizons; on 20+ year holds the impact is small.
